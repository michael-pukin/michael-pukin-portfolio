# Technical Research Narrative: Acrylic Surface Finishing for Lighting Applications

Context: Excerpt from a research and development project investigating methods for replicating a traditional Japanese-inspired textured finish on acrylic lighting products. Company-identifying information has been removed.

## Technical Uncertainties

In this project, we attempted to create a surface finish on acrylic material for lighting products that mimicked a traditional Japanese aesthetic featuring irregular, random texturing, which is traditionally done on wood using woodworking techniques. As acrylic has fundamentally different material properties than wood, traditional woodworking methods were not directly applicable, and no established techniques existed for creating this finish on acrylic. The project involved several interrelated technical uncertainties:

- Achieving a scalable production process for a traditionally hand-crafted aesthetic.
- Determining which tools and machining methods could successfully reproduce the texture in acrylic.
- Understanding how machining and surface treatment would affect light diffusion, colour temperature and LED concealment.
- Balancing aesthetic requirements with the functional requirements of a lighting product.

## Hand Tool Experimentation

We began our experimentation with hand tools similar to those used in the traditional woodworking techniques for creating the aesthetic. Using gouges and chisels, we attempted to see if we could approximate the handcrafted, irregular look of the aesthetic on acrylic material by hand. Results from this iteration were mixed; although there was some success mimicking the aesthetic, acrylic is by its nature much harder than wood and lacks grain, and this made controlled carving difficult. The labour-intensiveness, lack of consistency and difficult workability of acrylic material demonstrated that using hand tools would not be a solution for a repeatable manufacturing process for this purpose. This did establish an acceptable baseline for the textured aesthetic on acrylic.

## Router-Based Approaches

Observing that the properties of the acrylic material, such as the hardness and lack of grain, rendered the use of unmechanized hand tools slow and inefficient at consistently achieving the look for our purposes, we hypothesized that using large hand-operated, high-RPM routers may be more suitable for the process. We experimented with routers at RPMs between 8,000 and 22,000 to see if we could replicate the aesthetic, varying RPMs, router bits, pressures, and depths. In general, at all combinations of parameters, the handheld routers were efficient and quick at cutting into the acrylic material, but lacked required control for creating the desired textures, because these routers were not able to gimbal at severe enough arcs and thus could not replicate the nuanced, three-dimensional variations that are characteristic of the aesthetic; this was a problem especially with smaller pieces of acrylic. There was also a high degree of slippage of the acrylic material when working with it, and it often resulted in having to reclamp a piece mid-cut. The pressure of holding clamps also often resulted in marking of the acrylic. We could therefore rule out the use of large, hand-operated, high-RPM routers as a solution. 

Having established that the larger routers could achieve material more efficiently than hand tools but lacked adequate control when cutting tight angles, we hypothesized that using smaller routers might offer greater control. We employed smaller palm routers and designed custom jigs to hold the pieces while operating the routers to avoid slippage and marking of the acrylic. These iterations were more successful in cutting smaller arcs, and the custom jigs helped reduce slippage and marking. We found that at the lowest speed, 10,000 RPMs, we had the most success in replicating the texture. However, as with previous iterations, this process remained manual and not scalable for production.

## CNC Experimentation

In order to solve the problem of scalability and repeatability for production, we turned to programmable CNC machines to see if the creation of the textured aesthetic could be automated. Trials with the CNC machines were not successful in recreating the irregular, random textured look, and the torque applied by the machines also subjected the acrylic material to chipping, splitting and melting, and clamping and holding issues persisted as well. We concluded that standard CNC machining will not be suitable for this application.

# Lighting Performance Experiments

We also undertook experimentation in search of how to prepare the acrylic material for lighting performance in relation to light diffusion and colour temperature. We hypothesized that sandblasting the inside surface of the acrylic piece, combined with its textured outside, would achieve the desirable amount of light diffusion and visually obscure the LED diodes in the product. Sandblasting alone did not produce a sufficient amount of diffusion, so we tried adding varying opacities of paint to the inner surface. Adding the paint along with sandblasting was successful in terms of light diffusion, but introduced inconsistent colour temperature. The combination of these two treatments was partially successful in obscuring the LEDs.

We next surmised that applying layers of diffusion film to the inner surface of the acrylic piece might be superior at preserving colour temperature compared to the paint. After testing several films and evaluating them for diffusion and colour performance, thermal resistance and compatibility with the acrylic, we found a suitable diffusion film layer that successfully achieved desired light diffusion, colour temperature, and obscuring of the LEDs.

## Findings and Conclusions

As a result of our experimentation on this project, we determined that the desired textured aesthetic could be replicated on acrylic, but a scalable manufacturing process remained unresolved. Hand tools and palm routers produced the most promising visual results, while large routers and CNC machines lacked the control required to reproduce the organic texture and often damaged the material through excessive heat and torque.

We also determined that combining sandblasting with diffusion-film layers provided the most successful approach for light diffusion, colour-temperature preservation, and LED concealment. Future work will focus on identifying a repeatable and automated process capable of reproducing the aesthetic at production scale.
